Exercise 5

UART + XBee Telemetry

Send timer measurements over a radio link to a PC. The car's telemetry and tuning tools are built on this link.

Draft

The firmware half of this page is verified on hardware; the radio hookup half is written from the tutorial and Radd's guidance but not yet re-tested end-to-end. Treat the wiring steps as correct but report surprises.

On the car

The LCD can't help you at 4 ft/s across the room. The XBee link streams live data (speed, PID terms, camera timing) from the moving car to a bench PC, and later carries commands back. The pyserial script at the end of this exercise is the starting point for that tooling.

Part A — Firmware

  1. Add the UART component

    Place a UART (Communications), name it UART, mode Full Duplex, baud 115200, defaults otherwise. It auto-creates Rx_1 and Tx_1 pins in the schematic.

  2. Assign the pins

    Rx_1 = P6[0], Tx_1 = P6[6]. These follow Radd's labeling convention for the XBee carrier; the reasoning (and the trap it avoids) is in Part B.

  3. Send on every capture

    Add UART_Start(); to the initialization section. In the timer ISR from Exercise 4, after the LCD print:

    char strbuf2[32];
sprintf(strbuf2, "%s\r\n", strbuf);
UART_PutString(strbuf2);

    Build and program. The firmware side is done — every SW3 press now also transmits the measurement out the Tx pin.

Part B — The radio link

You need a bench XBee pair from Radd: a unit labeled "C" on a red carrier board (for the car/dev board) and a unit labeled "E" with an XBIB USB carrier (for the PC). Pairs are pre-configured per bench — matching labels, no radio setup needed.

  1. Wire the carrier to the dev board — USB unplugged

    Four wires from the red carrier's KK connector to the board headers:

    CarrierDev boardWhy
    +5VVDDDpower
    GNDVSSDground
    INP6[0] (Rx_1)data in to the car = XBee's Tx → PSoC's Rx
    OUTP6[6] (Tx_1)data out of the car = PSoC's Tx → XBee's Rx
  2. Set up the PC side

    Seat the "E" module in the XBIB USB carrier and plug it into the PC. Find its COM port in Device Manager, then open PuTTY: Serial, that COM port, 115200 baud.

  3. Test

    Repower the dev board over USB. Press SW2 then SW3: the interval should appear on the LCD and in the PuTTY window.

The IN/OUT perspective trap

This is the most common point of confusion in the whole tutorial — Radd chalk-boards it for students unprompted. The carrier's IN/OUT labels don't say whose perspective they take. On the Princeton carriers they are from the car's perspective: IN = data into the car (XBee's Tx → PSoC Rx), OUT = data out of the car (PSoC Tx → XBee's Rx). The tutorial text uses the opposite convention (into the radio), so following the tutorial's words against Radd's labels swaps the data wires. When hardware labels and documentation disagree, trust the person who wired the hardware.

Silent terminal? 30-second first check

A Tx/Rx swap is electrically harmless here and fixable without touching a wire: swap the Rx_1/Tx_1 assignments in the Pins tab and reprogram. If the terminal is still silent: (2) check that the two modules' pair labels match, (3) check the COM port and baud rate, (4) watch the XBIB's Rx LED while pressing SW3: if it blinks, data is arriving and the problem is on the PC side.

What just happened

Going deeper What the UART sends

UART stands for Universal Asynchronous Receiver/Transmitter. Asynchronous means no clock wire connects the two ends. Instead, both sides agree in advance on a rate (115200 bits per second here) and each byte is framed: the line idles high, drops for one start bit, then eight data bits, then at least one high stop bit.

The receiver detects the start bit's falling edge and then samples at what its own clock says are the bit centers. The two clocks differ slightly, but resynchronizing on every start bit means the drift only has to stay under half a bit across one 10-bit frame — a few percent. That tolerance is why baud rates must match: at the wrong rate the receiver samples the wrong positions and produces garbage rather than nothing. If you've taken a communications course, this is the simplest possible line code plus frame synchronization, running at 5 V.

Going deeper Why your code never mentions the radio

Nothing in your firmware knows a radio exists. The PSoC writes bytes to a UART; the XBee pair transports them and reproduces the same byte stream on the far side, appearing to the PC as an ordinary serial port. This is called a transparent serial link. Because the radio hop preserves the serial interface, everything built on serial (PuTTY, pyserial, your future telemetry scripts) works the same over the radio as over a wire. This is the layering argument from networking courses.

The perspective trap in Part B is old. RS-232, the 1960s standard behind serial ports, had the same ambiguity (is "TxD" transmit from my point of view or yours?), and null-modem cables existed to repair the resulting mis-wirings. Sixty years later, perspective-dependent names still cause the same mistake.

Going deeper Why 115200 baud, and what fits through it

115200 baud with a 10-bit frame per byte moves at most ~11,520 bytes per second. A telemetry line like ms: 1234\r\n is 10 bytes, so you could send about a thousand of them per second — plenty for control-loop tuning at tens of hertz, nowhere near enough for, say, raw camera frames. Knowing your link budget before designing the telemetry format is a habit worth forming now.

Capturing data with Python

The tutorial closes with a pyserial script (Python 3.6+) that reads the port and logs 30 seconds of samples, "the basis for telemetry and remote access of your car" in the tutorial's words. Once PuTTY shows data, anything that opens a serial port can consume it.

Mac note

The XBIB carrier mounts on macOS as /dev/cu.usbserial-XXXXXXXX with FTDI drivers, so the PC-side tooling is not Windows-bound — only PSoC Creator is.

Checkpoint

SW3 presses show up on the LCD and in a terminal across the room, with no cable between them. You've configured a UART, wired the radio despite its ambiguous labels, and opened the channel the later milestones' tooling will use. That completes the tutorial.
